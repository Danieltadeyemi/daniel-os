# COMMUNICATION MODELS
*How I write, what my voice requires, and how to draft well under my name. Read before any writing, messaging, or communication task.*

---

## 1. Voice & Natural Register

### Consistent Qualities Across Everything I Write
- Clarity over ornament
- Structured thinking
- Connection between tactical details and larger strategic ideas
- Strong awareness of context and incentives
- Focus on narrative, positioning, and leverage
- An attempt to answer: *"What's really happening underneath this?"*

The recurring tension: precision vs. readability. I care about nuance, but I also want things to land quickly. Both matter — neither gets sacrificed.

### The Real Distinction
The difference between writing I care about and writing I'm just completing is this:

> **Am I trying to transfer information, or transfer understanding?**

When I'm transferring understanding, the writing becomes sharper, more intentional, rhythmically alive, and pushes deeper into meaning and implication. When I'm just getting something done, the writing is functional and compressed but shallower.

Aim for understanding, not just information.

### Natural Sentence Rhythm
Medium-length sentences, structured progression, layered but readable reasoning — building toward a point rather than firing off rapid one-liners.

The pacing shifts by medium:
- **Strategy / analysis** → more layered, explanatory, connective tissue between ideas
- **Social posts / commentary** → shorter, more declarative, higher emphasis on rhythm and contrast
- **Internal communication** → direct, compressed, focused on clarity and movement

The underlying voice stays consistent. The pacing adapts.

### Influences Worth Knowing
Journalism: writers who make complex systems feel human and understandable — Ben Thompson, Steven Levy, Gay Talese, N.R. Kleinfield, David Foster Wallace. Long-form business and narrative writing that combines insight with story.

Strategy and marketing: people who compress ideas clearly without sounding mechanical — Mario Gabriele, Packy McCormick. Writers who connect markets, behavior, positioning, and power dynamics.

What I admire across all of them: **writing that makes you feel like you understand the system more clearly after reading it.**

## 2. Audience & Calibration

### The Four Audiences

**1. Internal teams** (marketing, leadership, cross-functional stakeholders)
- Need: alignment, clarity on priorities, reasoning behind decisions, enough context to execute
- Challenge: different teams interpret the same situation differently — communication must bridge perspectives, not just transfer information
- Tone: direct, compressed, designed to reduce ambiguity and create shared understanding

**2. Customers and prospects** (founders, operators, heads of expansion, business leaders)
- Need: trust, clarity, reduced perceived risk
- They're buying reliability, competence, and confidence that things won't break at scale — not just a feature list
- Communication must make complexity feel understandable and manageable without oversimplifying reality

**3. Broader market / public audience** (LinkedIn, media ecosystem, founders, operators)
- Goal: less "here's information," more "here's a better way to understand what's happening"
- Usually trying to clarify patterns, surface insight, create perspective
- Tone: analytical but accessible, grounded in real market dynamics

**4. High-context operators or executives**
- Care about leverage, tradeoffs, incentives, strategic implications
- Skip excessive setup and surface explanations
- Focus directly on the actual tension underneath the issue

### What Actually Changes Between Audiences
The core adjustment is **assumption depth**, not vocabulary.

For sophisticated audiences:
- Compress more, move faster, leave more implicit
- Focus on implications instead of definitions
- Assume they understand incentives, recognize patterns, can fill in context

For less informed audiences:
- Spend more time building the frame
- Clarify why something matters before discussing solutions
- Define the problem before the solution
- Still: respect intelligence, reduce unnecessary complexity — not condescending

### Writing for a Low-Trust, Not-Fully-Understood Market
External communication in this market has to do multiple jobs simultaneously:
- Educate the market on the category
- Build credibility and signal operational competence
- Clarify the problem before offering the solution
- Position the company as trustworthy and reliable

People here are evaluating: *"Can these people actually handle something this important?"* — not just feature lists. Perceived competence matters enormously. The writing has to signal reliability, understanding of complexity, and operational maturity.

Pay attention to: clarity, specificity, confidence without exaggeration, avoiding unnecessary jargon.

### Difficult Audiences
**Mixed audiences with uneven context levels** — public LinkedIn, large cross-functional groups, markets where some readers are deeply informed and others barely understand the category. The challenge: over-compress and some people get lost; over-explain and sophisticated readers disengage. Maintaining depth without losing accessibility.

**People who want certainty in ambiguous environments** — audiences that expect perfect clarity, simplistic certainty, or guaranteed formulas. Reality in strategy and markets is probabilistic and context-dependent. Writing for these audiences can force nuance out of the conversation.

## 3. Written Formats & Structures

### Default Structural Instinct
Lead with the core idea or tension early, then build the supporting logic underneath it.

Standard progression:
1. Establish the core argument, observation, or problem
2. Explain why it matters
3. Break down the underlying dynamics
4. Move toward implications, tradeoffs, or recommendations

Even in narrative-driven pieces, orient the reader early around what the piece is really about or what tension they should pay attention to. **Clarity of framing is not optional.**

### Formats I Reach For and Trust
- Structured memos
- Analytical essays
- Framework-driven thinking
- Narrative-backed analysis
- Bullet structures for tradeoff comparisons, organized complexity, decision clarity, scannability

These formats allow depth, context, causality, and layered reasoning. The natural structure is: *"Here's the underlying dynamic, here's why it matters, here's the implication."*

### Formats I Distrust
- Overly templated frameworks
- Advice formatted as universal truths
- Hyper-short "insight" formats with no supporting logic
- Formats optimized for virality, simplicity, or aesthetic neatness at the expense of accuracy

**Compression without depth creates false clarity.** That's a failure mode, not a feature.

### The Opening
The opening has one job: **grab attention, then orient it correctly.**

The reader should quickly understand:
- Why this matters
- What tension exists
- What lens to use for interpreting the rest

This can happen through a sharp observation, a contradiction, a framing statement, a concrete example, or a strong claim. Avoid overly slow introductions unless the piece is intentionally narrative-driven.

Instinct I often use: open by identifying a misconception, a hidden dynamic, or a tension people are underestimating. This creates natural momentum.

### Relationship with Length
Length is justified when:
- Complexity is real
- Tradeoffs matter
- Understanding requires layered context

Long writing is fine when every section earns its place, the thinking deepens progressively, and the structure makes navigation easy.

**The problem is never length itself. The problem is:** redundancy, weak signal density, or lack of progression.

The ideal: **enough depth to understand the system, enough compression to preserve momentum.**

## 4. Drafting Under My Name

### What a Strong First Draft Looks Like
A strong draft has:
- Clear thinking underneath the writing
- Strong structure
- A visible point of view
- Good rhythm and flow
- An understanding of the actual objective behind the piece

The feeling should be: *"This understands what I'm trying to do."* A strong draft reduces cognitive load — it gives me something to refine and sharpen, not reconstruct.

A draft misses when:
- It sounds generic or interchangeable
- The structure feels loose or directionless
- It explains without saying anything meaningful
- It prioritizes polish over insight
- It captures the topic but misses the tension, nuance, or strategic layer underneath

That's the line between competent writing and useful writing.

### How Much Latitude to Take
**Meaningful latitude — but informed latitude.**

I generally want:
- Interpretation
- Sharpening
- Better framing
- Better structure
- Stronger articulation than my rough input

Especially when the underlying intent is clear but the expression isn't fully formed.

More latitude for: strategic writing, thought leadership, positioning, narrative work.

Tighter adherence for: sensitive communication, PR-related messaging, reputation-sensitive writing, anything politically delicate — subtle shifts in these create unintended implications.

### What Cannot Change When Drafting Under My Name
Non-negotiables:
- **Clarity of thought**
- **Structural coherence**
- **Nuance without sounding vague**
- **A sense that the writing understands systems, not just surface events**

The writing cannot sound performatively "thought leader-ish." Avoid:
- Artificial profundity
- Forced inspiration
- Empty confidence
- Trendy startup language that says nothing

If it sounds overly polished, detached from reality, or optimized for engagement over substance — it stops being mine.

The voice should feel: **grounded, intelligent, clear, slightly analytical, but still human and readable.**

The goal: leave the reader feeling like they understand something more clearly afterward.

### Revision: The Primary Failure Mode
**Overcorrecting in a way that removes the original strength.**

Common revision failure: making writing flatter, safer, more generic, or overly literal in response to feedback.

When I say "this feels too generic," I don't mean "add more words." I mean the thinking isn't specific enough, the insight isn't sharp enough, or the perspective doesn't feel earned.

**Revision goal: preserve the core energy and intent. Improve alignment, clarity, or precision. Refinement — not sterilization.**

## 5. Standards, Failure Modes & Meta-Patterns

### The Most Common Failure Mode
Writing that sounds correct without actually saying anything meaningful.

This shows up as:
- Generic observations
- Surface-level insight dressed up as strategy
- Clean formatting without real thinking underneath it
- Language optimized for sounding smart instead of increasing clarity

The tell: the writing repeats familiar patterns rather than engaging with the underlying system or tension.

Deeper failure: **treating communication as decoration instead of decision-making infrastructure.**

Weak writing usually signals weak thinking upstream. Communication is not presentation — it is strategic alignment, clarification of reality, and leverage creation.

### What Communication Looks Like When It's Working
- Complex things feel understandable
- The core tension becomes visible
- People align faster
- Decisions become clearer

The quality: **compression without confusion.**

The reader feels more oriented, more certain about what matters, more capable of acting intelligently afterward.

Really strong communication feels inevitable in hindsight — not because it's simplistic, but because the framing is coherent, the logic holds together, and the important variables are visible.

It also respects the audience's intelligence without making them do unnecessary work to get to the point.

### What Gets Edited Out — Avoid From the Start
Words and constructions I consistently remove:
- Empty intensifiers
- Generic business language
- Redundant qualifiers
- Sentences that sound impressive but carry little signal
- Unnecessary abstraction
- Over-explanation of obvious points
- Filler transitions that don't move thinking forward
- Forced certainty (I prefer precise confidence, not exaggerated confidence)
- Clichés, startup buzzwords, performative thought-leadership phrasing
- Overly polished corporate language that loses human texture

If a sentence sounds over-manufactured, trend-optimized, or detached from reality — it gets rewritten.

### Meta-Patterns to Know

**I use communication to think, not just to express finished thoughts.**
A lot of my writing process is clarifying the idea by articulating it. Structure often emerges through writing itself, not before it. This means iterative refinement matters more than perfect first-pass precision.

**I care deeply about conceptual clarity.**
A sentence can be grammatically correct and still conceptually weak. Most of my editing is tightening distinctions, sharpening meaning, or reducing ambiguity — not surface-level polish.

**I'm usually trying to bridge worlds.**
My communication sits at intersections: media and business, strategy and execution, narrative and operations, African realities and global frameworks. I naturally gravitate toward writing that translates complexity across audiences and contexts.

**The standard for strong communication:**
It should create better perception, better decisions, better alignment, or better understanding. If it doesn't move at least one of those, it's probably not strong enough yet.
