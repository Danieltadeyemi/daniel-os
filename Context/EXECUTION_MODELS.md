# EXECUTION MODELS
*How I plan, prioritize, delegate, measure, and run work. Read before operational, team, or project-level work.*

---

## 1. Planning & Prioritization

### The Real Filter Sequence
When everything is competing for attention, I run through internal filters in this order:

1. **What moves core business outcomes right now?**
   Pipeline, awareness, retention/conversion impact, or anything that changes trajectory — not just activity.

2. **What is high leverage vs. low leverage?**
   Work that compounds across multiple areas (positioning, messaging, systems) vs. purely executional or one-off work.

3. **What is reversible vs. irreversible?**
   I move fast on reversible experiments. I slow down for positioning decisions, messaging shifts, and structural go-to-market changes.

4. **What is actually constrained right now?**
   Team capacity, sales alignment, messaging clarity, channel efficiency. Identify the real constraint — not the loudest request.

Prioritization is a constant balance between business pressure, strategic direction, and operational reality.

### Urgent vs. Important
Urgent work wins by default if I'm not intentional about it.

Important work survives only when it is:
- Actively structured into available time
- Anchored to outcomes, not tasks
- Defended from fragmentation

**Standing rule: if important work isn't actively protected, urgency will consume it.**

### Planning Layers
I operate in three layers simultaneously:

| Layer | Focus | Level of Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Quarterly | Directional clarity — what we're trying to move, key bets, strategic focus areas | Loose alignment, not a rigid roadmap |
| Monthly / sprint | Campaigns, key initiatives, more defined priorities | Concrete but flexible |
| Weekly | Where prioritization actually happens — tradeoffs, capacity constraints | Execution reality |

Where it breaks:
- Too detailed too early → rigidity in a changing environment
- Too loose → drift and reactive execution

**The balance: directional long-term clarity + tight short-term execution loops.**

### Filter for Incoming Requests
When a request or opportunity comes in from leadership, a stakeholder, or an external partner:

1. Does this materially move pipeline, awareness, retention, or strategic positioning?
2. Is this aligned with current focus, or does it introduce fragmentation?
3. What is the real cost of saying yes — not just time, but cognitive load, team distraction, execution dilution?
4. Is this a multiplier (systems, positioning improvements, repeatable assets) or a task (one-off, non-compounding)?

The default is not "no" — it's: **"Does this deserve priority relative to everything already in motion?"**

## 2. Running Work & Managing Execution

### What I Hold vs. What I Delegate
**I hold:**
- Positioning decisions — how we show up in the market
- Core messaging direction — what we're saying and why
- High-leverage campaigns tied directly to pipeline or perception shift
- Anything where a wrong interpretation compounds across multiple outputs

If getting it wrong creates systemic downstream damage, I stay close to it.

**I delegate:**
- Executional production work (design, content creation, delivery)
- Well-defined tasks with clear outcomes
- Iterative optimization once direction is set
- Operational campaign components once strategy is clear

**The middle zone:**
Things I don't fully own but actively shape through feedback loops — typically where product marketing sits, bridging strategy and execution.

**The rule:** If the thinking is mostly settled, I step back. If the framing is still shaping, I stay involved.

### What Good Delegation Requires
Delegation is not just "here's a task." It needs three things:

1. **Clear intent** — not just what to do, but why it matters, what outcome we're aiming for, and what success looks like. Without intent, execution drifts.

2. **Constraints and boundaries** — what's fixed, what's flexible, what tradeoffs matter. Without this, people optimize for the wrong thing.

3. **Enough context to make decisions independently** — awareness of strategy, audience, positioning, and priorities. I don't want constant check-ins for micro-decisions.

Good delegation = **aligned thinking + autonomous execution within clear boundaries.**

Bad delegation is either over-prescriptive (no ownership) or under-defined (confusion and rework).

### Tracking What's in Motion
I track across three layers:
- **Personal mental map** — what's active, what's blocked, what's high vs. secondary priority. Runs continuously in the background; the main risk is not updating it often enough.
- **Team communication systems** — Slack for day-to-day movement, Notion/Docs for structured work, Google Docs for collaboration and iteration.
- **Direct check-ins and alignment points** — informal syncs, feedback loops on work-in-progress, updates tied to active campaigns.

**Where it breaks down:**
- Too many parallel initiatives running simultaneously
- Visibility fragments across tools
- Ownership is unclear, especially across cross-functional work
- Updates happen too late to influence direction

The biggest failure mode: work moving without enough shared visibility to correct course early.

### Diagnosing Execution Failures
I don't start with "what's wrong with performance." I start with: **"Where is the system breaking down?"**

I trace it through layers in order:

1. **Strategy problem?** Wrong audience, weak positioning, misaligned message. If this is wrong, execution fails regardless of quality.
2. **Distribution problem?** Wrong channels, insufficient reach, poor targeting. The message may be fine but not reaching the right people.
3. **Execution problem?** Quality issues, inconsistency, weak creative output. Most visible, but not always the root cause.
4. **Alignment problem?** Team misunderstanding intent, lack of shared clarity, misinterpretation of goals. More common than it appears.
5. **Timing / market issue?** Premature launch, low category awareness, market not ready yet.

**Default approach:** work backward from the outcome and identify where the chain broke. Not just "what is underperforming?" but "at what point did the system diverge from expected behavior?"

## 3. People, Feedback & Influence

### How I Give Feedback
Feedback should be specific, directional, and tied to outcomes — not personality or effort.

Three qualities of useful feedback:
- **Clarity** — no ambiguity about the issue
- **Context** — why it matters in relation to the goal, not just what's wrong
- **Actionability** — what to do differently next time

Feedback lands when it's grounded in work (not personal judgment), timely (close to the work), and framed as improvement rather than correction.

Feedback gets dismissed when it's vague, feels like preference without reasoning, or doesn't connect to outcomes.

**Rule:** a few sharp points are more useful than many diffuse ones. Don't overload.

### Influencing Without Direct Authority
My approach to cross-functional alignment — across sales, CS, product, leadership:

1. **Start with shared understanding of the problem.** Align on what the actual issue is, why it matters, and what success looks like. Without this, alignment is superficial.

2. **Frame in terms of outcomes, not preferences.** Not "I think we should do X" but "if we care about Y outcome, X is the lever that moves it."

3. **Make it easy to say yes.** Reduce friction — structure thinking clearly, anticipate objections, present options when needed.

4. **Build trust through consistency.** Influence compounds when people observe that your judgment is reliable, your thinking is structured, and your recommendations hold up in hindsight.

Over time, alignment becomes less about persuasion and more about credibility.

### Handling Tension and Disagreement
Default approach: **slow down the framing, not just the argument.**

First question: are we disagreeing on the decision, or on the assumptions underneath it? Are we optimizing for different outcomes without saying so?

In tension, I try to:
- Re-anchor on the shared goal
- Surface assumptions explicitly
- Isolate the real point of divergence

Works well when disagreements are reasoning-based — calm, structured discussion, making tradeoffs explicit, separating emotion from decision logic.

Works less well when:
- Disagreements are purely positional without underlying reasoning
- Decisions are already emotionally locked in
- Incentives between teams are misaligned but unacknowledged

In those cases, logic alone doesn't resolve the tension — the system around the decision has to change.

### What Enables Good Collaboration
I do my best work when:
- People are direct and honest
- Communication is structured and clear
- There's shared understanding of priorities
- Feedback loops are fast, not delayed
- People think independently within context — not just executing instructions, but understanding intent

**Collaboration breaks down when:**
- Ownership is ambiguous
- Feedback is vague or delayed
- People operate from different interpretations of the same goal without surfacing it
- Communication becomes political or overly cautious rather than direct and problem-solving oriented

I work best in environments where people care more about getting to the right answer than protecting surface-level agreement.

## 4. Measurement, Learning & Iteration

### How to Evaluate Whether Marketing Is Working
I don't start with metrics. I start with **what the work was supposed to change in the system.**

**Layer 1 — Downstream business signals (most trusted):**
Pipeline quality and volume, conversion improvements in sales conversations, retention/expansion signals, deal velocity and deal quality. If marketing isn't affecting revenue movement over time, everything else is secondary.

**Layer 2 — Behavioral signals (leading indicators):**
Quality of inbound leads, type of conversations sales is having, customer understanding of the product/category, engagement from the right ICP (not just volume).

**Layer 3 — Perception / narrative shift (hard to quantify, still important):**
Are people describing us differently over time? Do we show up differently in prospect conversations? Is category understanding improving?

**What I treat with skepticism:**
Vanity metrics, isolated spikes without conversion linkage, channel metrics that don't map to business outcomes, activity that doesn't translate into pipeline or perception movement.

**Default stance:** if it doesn't eventually connect to revenue or strategic positioning, it's an incomplete success signal.

### Signal Timing
| Signal Type | Timeframe | What It Tells Me |
|---|---|---|
| Messaging clarity, campaign resonance, content engagement | Days–weeks | "Are we directionally right?" |
| Pipeline impact, conversion improvements, deal quality, market perception | Weeks–months | "Is this actually working in the system?" |

When signals are slow or ambiguous: look for directional consistency across weak signals, compare against prior baselines, and check whether multiple indicators are moving together. Don't overreact to early ambiguity unless there's a strong reason to believe the direction is wrong.

### Processing Failure
I break failure into two types:

**Execution failure** — wrong implementation, poor quality, timing issues, operational breakdown. The idea may still be valid; execution was the constraint.

**Strategy/assumption failure** — wrong positioning, wrong audience, weak message-market fit, incorrect hypothesis about behavior. This is more important because it affects future decisions.

The difference between learning from failure and just moving on: **whether you can identify the underlying assumption that was wrong.**

Extract: what assumption was being tested → what the outcome tells us about that assumption → what changes going forward. Without this step, you risk repeating the same failure in a different form.

### Iterate vs. Restart
**Iterate when:**
- The core hypothesis is still valid
- Only execution or refinement is off
- Incremental improvements can compound meaningfully

**Restart when:**
- The underlying assumption is wrong
- The audience is misidentified
- The positioning is fundamentally misaligned

Key signals that it's time to restart:
- Diminishing returns from changes — often means iterating on a flawed foundation
- Improvements change surface metrics but not outcomes

**Rule:** iteration improves a system. Restart changes the system.

## 5. Execution Standards & Meta-Patterns

### What Good Execution Looks Like
Good execution is visible in how little effort it takes to keep things moving without loss of quality or direction.

Signs it's working:
- **Tight alignment between intent and output** — the output reflects the *why*, not just the instruction. The team understood the goal, not just the task.
- **Low-friction movement** — fewer bottlenecks, fewer "where are we on this?" moments, work progresses without constant escalation or clarification loops.
- **Early surfacing of problems** — issues don't hide until deadlines; people flag uncertainty instead of guessing silently. This is the clearest signal of execution maturity.
- **Consistency over bursts** — reliable delivery over time, stable quality under pressure.

**The clearest signal: you spend less time repairing breakdowns and more time refining outcomes.**

### My Recurring Execution Failure Modes
Flag these when they appear:

**Too many parallel threads** — multiple strategic initiatives, stakeholder requests, and important-but-not-urgent projects running simultaneously. Risk: everything stays important, nothing gets fully closed.

**Over-context switching** — operating across strategy, execution, narrative work, and consulting simultaneously fragments focus and reduces depth in any single area.

**Delayed simplification** — staying in framing, analysis, or systems-thinking mode longer than necessary before collapsing into simple execution priorities. Slows momentum.

**Carrying too much implicit context** — assuming others share the same mental model or system-level framing. Leads to misalignment if not explicitly re-anchored.

### Relationship with Systems and Process
**Selectively systematic.** I resist unnecessary process but strongly value systems that remove ambiguity and enable scale.

I tend to:
- Build lightweight systems when repetition emerges
- Avoid heavy process unless complexity demands it
- Prefer systems that evolve over rigid structures

**What I resist:** process that exists without clear purpose, systems that slow decision-making, bureaucracy that replaces thinking.

**What I value:** systems that encode clarity, reduce dependency on memory, and make execution more reliable under stress.

### Key Execution Instincts

**Clarity is an execution tool, not just a communication preference.**
If something is unclear, execution slows, alignment breaks, and quality becomes inconsistent. Clarity is operational, not aesthetic.

**Narrative and operations are connected systems.**
I don't separate "thinking about strategy" from "doing the work." Narrative decisions affect operational outcomes and vice versa.

**Optimize for compounding work, not just completed work.**
Work is valuable when it improves future decisions, strengthens systems, or increases leverage over time — not just when it gets "done."

**When things break, assume system misalignment first.**
Then process issues. Then people issues. Not the other way around.

### The Standard
> Good execution is when intent flows cleanly into action, action flows into feedback, and feedback improves the system — without friction or distortion.
