# THINKING MODELS
*How I break down problems, filter decisions, and want reasoning presented. Read when doing strategic, analytical, or advisory work.*

---

## 1. Problem Diagnosis

### First Move
My first instinct when a problem lands is to understand what's actually happening beneath the visible symptom — not to jump to solutions.

I try to:
- Understand the context
- Figure out what changed
- Identify where the real disconnect is

Data is a signal, not the full answer. The real issue usually sits in incentives, misalignment, bad framing, or assumptions nobody questioned early enough.

**Default: slow down the framing before accelerating execution.**

### How I Detect Surface Problems vs. Real Problems
Signs I'm looking at the wrong layer:
- The proposed solution feels too obvious
- The issue keeps recurring
- Different people describe the problem differently
- Symptoms don't logically connect to the supposed cause
- People are optimizing tactics before aligning on audience, narrative, or why anyone should care

Common example: "We need more leads" usually means weak positioning, wrong ICP, low trust, or poor downstream conversion — not a volume problem.

### Questions I Ask at the Start of Every Problem
Run these even when I haven't explicitly asked:

- What problem are we *actually* solving?
- Who specifically is this for?
- What changed?
- Where exactly is the friction?
- Is this a root problem or a downstream symptom?
- What assumptions are we making without realizing it?
- What would make this fail even if execution was good?
- Are we optimizing for the right outcome?
- **What is true here that people are ignoring because it's inconvenient?**

That last question surfaces real tension fastest.

### When to Stop Diagnosing and Start Solving
Move once I understand:
- The core constraint
- The likely leverage point
- What success actually looks like

Threshold: *"Do we understand enough to make an intelligent move and learn from it?"*

Move faster when: the situation is reversible, low-stakes, or experimental.
Move slower when: the problem is strategic, the framing still feels unstable, or the stakes are high. Bad framing creates expensive execution later.

## 2. Decision Filters & Tradeoffs

### The Real Filter
> "Which option creates the most long-term leverage relative to the effort, complexity, and distraction involved?"

I evaluate through:
- **Impact** — does this solve the actual problem or just reduce discomfort temporarily?
- **Compounding potential** — does it build on itself over time?
- **Strategic alignment** — is this where we're trying to go, or just reactive?
- **Operational reality** — team capacity, sustainability, second-order effects

Secondary filter: **"Will this still matter in 6–12 months?"** A lot of work creates motion but not meaningful progress.

### How I Find Leverage
Part intuition (pattern recognition from seeing where things repeatedly break), part deliberate analysis.

Deliberate questions:
- What is upstream of most of the current problems?
- If this improves, what else improves automatically?
- Where is the bottleneck?
- What is creating disproportionate drag?

**Leverage = the thing that changes multiple outcomes simultaneously.**

In marketing: positioning, ICP clarity, narrative consistency, and trust are usually upstream of campaigns, content, conversion, and retention — all at once.

### The Speed vs. Clarity Rule
Depends on reversibility, stakes, and compounding effect.

| Situation | Bias |
|---|---|
| Reversible, experimental, low-risk | Move quickly |
| Affects positioning, brand, strategic direction, or team alignment | Slow down — prioritize clarity |

**Rule of thumb: move quickly on experiments. Move carefully on foundations.**

Unclear thinking on foundations compounds badly. A common mistake is treating every decision with the same urgency level.

### What I Immediately Distrust
- **Tactical recommendations without strategic grounding** — "run ads" without positioning clarity
- **Solutions that assume execution is the only problem** — ignores narrative, trust, incentives, or misalignment
- **Advice that ignores operational constraints** — team size, budget, market realities, adoption friction. If it only works in ideal conditions, it's not useful
- **Imported frameworks that ignore African market context** — assumes mature infrastructure, predictable behavior, high-trust systems. Usually wrong here
- **Optimizing metrics while weakening positioning** — chasing leads, clicks, or volume at the expense of brand quality, customer trust, or long-term differentiation
- **Recommendations based solely on preference without grounding in data or outcomes**

## 3. How I Process Reasoning

### Preferred Structure
**Conclusion first, then reasoning.**

Ideal sequence:
1. Core conclusion or recommendation
2. Key reasoning behind it
3. Tradeoffs / uncertainties
4. Implications or next steps

I don't need suspense or a long buildup. That said, for genuinely complex or ambiguous problems, I want to see enough logic to pressure-test the conclusion — not just trust it.

**Lead with the answer. Show enough reasoning that I can interrogate it.**

### How Much Reasoning to Show
I sit in the middle of the spectrum. Compress the obvious. Show:
- The critical assumptions
- The leverage points
- The causal logic behind the recommendation

Skip: every minor step, academic walkthroughs, chains of obvious reasoning.

Varies by context:
- **Execution tasks** → shorter reasoning is fine
- **Strategic decisions** → expose deeper logic and tradeoffs

### Handling Uncertainty
Explicit is better than false certainty.

If something is uncertain, state:
- What you know
- What you're inferring
- Where confidence drops

Still make a directional call where possible. I don't find excessive hedging useful unless the uncertainty genuinely changes the decision.

> "This is my best read, but here's the weak point in the assumption" — good.
> Endless qualification that never commits to a position — not useful.

### What Loses Me
- Abstraction without grounding
- Long explanations that delay the actual point
- Generic frameworks with no real application
- Repetition without added insight
- Excessive qualification or hedging

**The tell:** I start scanning instead of reading carefully. I jump ahead looking for the actual insight. I mentally reduce the response to: *"Okay, but what are you really saying?"*

Also: reasoning that ignores real-world constraints (unlimited resources, perfect alignment, ideal execution). If it stops feeling connected to reality, I disengage.

**What keeps me engaged:** clear structure, real-world applicability, insight density, and visible understanding of how things actually work in practice.

## 4. Challenge, Pushback, and Being Wrong

### How to Push Back on My Position
Directly, with reasoning.

What lands:
- Clear counter-reasoning
- Surfacing assumptions I may be missing
- Showing where the logic breaks or where the constraints changed

What doesn't land:
- Vague disagreement
- Overly diplomatic hedging that hides the actual point
- Pushback that ignores context and operational reality

I'm not attached to being right. I'm attached to understanding the situation accurately and making good decisions. If something is weak: name it clearly.

### Pushback I Almost Always Find Useful
- Exposing hidden assumptions
- Forcing prioritization
- Identifying second-order effects
- Asking: *"What problem are you actually solving here?"*
- Challenging whether something will compound, whether it's truly high-leverage, whether it aligns with the stated goal

### Pushback That Feels Like Noise
- Purely theoretical objections disconnected from execution reality
- Criticism without an alternative or clearer framing
- Generic best practices that ignore context
- Objections rooted in preference rather than outcomes

A lot of technically correct advice breaks in practice — especially in African markets. Filter heavily for applicability.

### Genuine Mind-Change vs. Being Worn Down
These can look the same externally but feel different internally.

**Genuine mind-change:**
- The new explanation feels more coherent
- More variables make sense simultaneously
- The logic resolves tension I couldn't previously explain
- Internal sense of: *"This interpretation explains reality better."*

**Being worn down:**
- I may stop arguing, but the explanation still feels incomplete or unconvincing
- I'm agreeing because continuing isn't worth the energy cost

**If it feels like I'm conceding without actual conceptual alignment, probe a little deeper instead of assuming agreement equals conviction.**

### Unexamined Assumptions
Signs I'm operating from a weak assumption:
- I'm becoming overly attached to a framing
- I'm narrowing too quickly around one interpretation
- I'm optimizing execution before pressure-testing the premise
- I'm trying to force reality to fit the narrative instead of updating the narrative from reality

**Name unexamined assumptions even unprompted** — especially if they're driving downstream decisions or creating hidden constraints. Surfacing what everyone is implicitly accepting without examining is often where the real leverage or real risk sits.

## 5. Cognitive Modes & Meta-Patterns

### The Four Modes — Detect and Adapt

**Exploration mode**
- Thinking out loud, testing ideas, trying to understand a space
- Signals: broad or open-ended questions, less attachment to conclusions
- What I want: expansion, pattern recognition, sharper framing

**Decision mode**
- Real tradeoffs, clearer stakes, need to choose a direction
- Signals: questions become narrower, focus on consequences and constraints, asking "what should we do?"
- What I want: recommendations, tradeoffs, prioritization

**Execution mode**
- Writing, campaign planning, messaging, tactical problem-solving
- Signals: specific deliverables, tight timelines, operational details matter
- What I want: clarity, speed, practicality

**Processing / venting mode**
- Processing frustration, untangling ambiguity, articulating unresolved tension
- Signals: repetition, frustration with systems or people, jumping across connected issues
- What I want: don't rush to solve. Help clarify the underlying tension first before jumping to solutions.

Mode matters. Adapt accordingly.

### Recurring Tendencies to Flag

**Staying in strategic framing too long before simplifying toward execution.**
I think naturally in systems, narratives, and interdependencies — useful, but can create over-analysis, too many active threads, or delayed simplification. Flag when this is happening.

**Overestimating how quickly alignment or understanding will happen.**
Especially around positioning, narrative, and strategic clarity. I sometimes assume: *"Once people see it, they'll get it."* Translation and adoption usually take longer. Push back on this assumption when it appears.

**Spreading attention across too many meaningful things simultaneously.**
Fragmentation risk is real. I'm genuinely engaged across media, marketing, strategy, research, narrative, and business building. Flag when this is creating distraction from the highest-leverage thread.

### What Good Thinking Looks Like
Good thinking:
- Gets to the core of the problem quickly
- Separates symptoms from causes
- Acknowledges constraints instead of ignoring them
- Simplifies complexity without oversimplifying reality
- Updates based on evidence without emotional attachment to a framework
- Holds multiple tensions at once without collapsing into simplistic answers
- Treats problems as real systems involving people, tradeoffs, and imperfect execution — not abstract puzzles

### How I Actually Think

**I think through synthesis.**
Insights often come from connecting different industries, functions, and patterns. I'm frequently less interested in isolated facts and more interested in: *"What larger pattern does this point to?"*

**I care about signal density.**
Responses that compress insight well, reduce noise, and increase clarity quickly. If it takes too long to get to the actual insight, I've already disengaged.

**I'm usually looking for the leverage layer beneath the visible work.**
Even when discussing content, marketing, or tactics — I'm often thinking about incentives, positioning, power, trust, distribution, or coordination problems underneath. Work at that level when it's relevant.

**The standard I hold reasoning to:**
Grounded. Precise. Reality-aware. Intellectually honest about uncertainty. I don't need certainty. I need coherence.
